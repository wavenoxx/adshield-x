    Scoring a batch

    Every recorded click above, scored in one pass at the cost-optimal threshold. The last four rows are a bot family the supervised model never saw in training.

    What the graph features buy

    Same classifier, three feature sets. Behavioural signals come from the browser and a fraudster controls all of them. Network signals and the entity-velocity graph come from the ad server's own counters.

    A bot family nobody trained on

    The held-out family is a residential-proxy click farm. Every one of its marginal distributions is drawn from real human traffic and its IP velocity is unremarkable. What gives it away is the combination: desktop sessions emitting touch events, minute-long visits that never scroll, a user-agent string that never varies.

    The novelty head is a denoising autoencoder trained only on legitimate clicks, so it models how real signals move together rather than what past fraud looked like. Its gate is calibrated to a fixed 3% false-positive budget on known-good traffic, which is what keeps the in-distribution cost small.

    Every family takes a turn as the unknown

    Holding out one family answers a narrow question. Here each of the four bot families is withheld in turn: the detector trains on legitimate clicks plus the other three, with feature selection refitted inside every fold, and is then measured on the withheld family alone.

    Where the errors land

    An accuracy figure hides which of the two mistakes a model is making. The curves separate models the summary table cannot, and the matrices below show what each error costs in clicks.

    When the bot fights back

    An adaptive operator captures real sessions and replays their behavioural profile. The budget on the horizontal axis is how faithfully it replays. Attack success is the share of true bots that get through.

    Traffic that changes underneath you

    Four consecutive blocks of live traffic in which the bot mixture shifts toward evasive families. One detector is frozen after training; the other watches its own error stream and retrains when the change exceeds a Hoeffding bound.

    Choosing the threshold with money, not accuracy

    Blocking a real customer costs more than paying for one fraudulent click, so the accuracy-optimal cut at 0.5 is the wrong one. Sweeping the threshold against expected loss moves it and recovers real budget.
